\title{ZPDPatch: Trading Repair Coverage for Student Code in Programming-Exercise Feedback}
\author{}
\begin{document}
\maketitle
\begin{abstract}
We present \textsc{ZPDPatch}, a trajectory-supervised technique that aims repair feedback at a student's own code by constructing targets from execution evidence rather than from accepted solutions. A repair returned to a student is feedback, so how much of their program it replaces matters alongside whether the tests pass. We call repair whose supervision depends on that history \emph{trajectory-conditioned}, and no single comparison establishes a benefit for it, because more candidates alone improve a history-aware system and a new target rule also changes the training population. Our goal is to separate the target rule's effect from those of extra candidates and serialized history. \textsc{ZPDPatch} mines three execution-evidence relations, trains independent adapters, and selects a validation-frozen portfolio behind a gate that returns the submission unchanged rather than a regression. On 181 student-held-out Introductory Programming (CS1) Java trajectories under source- and count-matched training, terminal targets repair 155 and progress targets 92, leading on 15 of 17 exercises. On the 90 trajectories both rules repair, the retention advantage is zero for 63.3\% of students, positive for 31.1\%, and negative for 5.6\%. An online-judge corpus of 997 inputs from problems seen in training and 250 from held-out problems reproduces the tradeoff, with 19.2 and 11.2 percentage points less coverage and 5.6 and 6.5 points more retained tokens. Three fixed candidate draws alone add 15.1 points of repair rate, and these numbers describe the feedback, not learning. We therefore retain the current-only terminal portfolio.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Automated Program Repair (APR)~\cite{gazzola2019automatic,monperrus2018automatic,weimer2009automatically,legoues2012genprog} has been widely studied for programming assignments as a way to give every rejected submission feedback that a course cannot write by hand~\cite{keuning2018systematic}. Given a buggy submission and the problem's test suite, a technique returns a patch, that is, a program that passes every test, so the student receives a working version of their own assignment~\cite{joshi2023repair,zhang2024pydex}. To this end, a technique decides which part of the submission to change, synthesizes a replacement from the material it is given, and returns only a candidate that the tests accept. A student submits repeatedly to the same assignment, so the technique faces a sequence rather than one buggy program~\cite{duan2024tiktoc}.

The key design decision is what that material is, that is, what the repair is supervised toward. Existing techniques for programming assignments fall into three groups by where they take it from. Alignment-based techniques align the submission with other students' passing programs or an instructor's solution~\cite{gulwani2018automated,wang2018search,hu2020re,heo2023referent,choi2021automated,choi2025automated}; language-model-based techniques generate a patch from the buggy program and the problem statement~\cite{joshi2023repair,zhang2024pydex,phung2023generating,liu2024fastfixer,yang2024cref,zhao2024peer,orvalho2025counterexample}; and retrieval-based techniques look up a learner-specific solution to steer that generation~\cite{dai2026learner}. In addition, next-step hint generators draw one edit from a solution space built out of past correct programs~\cite{piech2015embeddings,rivers2017datadriven,price2017isnap,roest2024next}. These techniques have raised the repair rate for student submissions considerably, and put a next step within reach of more students than a tutor can see~\cite{keuning2018systematic,koutcheme2024benchmarking}.

However, existing techniques do not know how far a student can get with guidance. Where a return aims is settled without reference to which tests the student currently passes. Feedback works better when it is specific to the learner's own work~\cite{hattie2007power,shute2008focus}, and it helps only as far as the student can follow the change~\cite{narciss2008feedback,aleven2000limitations}. The Zone of Proximal Development divides a learner's ability into what they can do unaided, what they can do with guidance, and what stays out of reach, and support has to land in the middle region~\cite{cole1978mind}. A submission is what the student produced unaided, so it marks where that first region ends, and feedback chosen without regard to the student's level can land anywhere beyond it. Locating those regions takes measurement of its own~\cite{murray2002toward,chounta2017grey,schulze2024beyond}, but the distance between a return and the submission is observable (Section~\ref{sec:limitation}). An average return of the most recent technique, LSGen~\cite{dai2026learner}, is 2.5 times an average student step. Consequently, existing techniques hand back somebody else's finished solution rather than the next step the student could take. In this work, we define \emph{trajectory-conditioned repair} as repair whose supervision depends on the sequence leading to the current submission.

In this paper, we present \textsc{ZPDPatch}, a new trajectory-supervised technique that repairs a buggy submission toward the next step the student could reach with guidance. Our key idea is that the student's own trajectory already records that step, since the submission that actually came next is a program the student reached unaided soon after. This target cannot be swapped in on its own (Section~\ref{sec:limitation}), so \textsc{ZPDPatch} holds the sources, the example counts and the candidate budget fixed and adds a matched-peer control. To this end, \textsc{ZPDPatch} operates in three stages, Mine, Train, and Select. The Mine stage pairs a buggy submission, or a retained prefix, with a later submission under three rules. Progress takes the next state that improves the verdict or passes more tests, Strict the next better-verdict state, and Answer the accepted endpoint. Unlike retrieval-based techniques that mine the same pairs to look up a solution, these pairs supply the supervision signal itself. The Train stage trains an independent adapter per rule. The Select stage freezes a triple of checkpoints by their executed validation repairs rather than by training loss. As a result, what a student receives is a complete program, so how much of their own submission it replaces can be measured and bounded.

Experimental results demonstrate that \textsc{ZPDPatch} changes the least of a student's program among the techniques we compare, and under a bounded edit repairs more submissions than LSGen. We evaluated it on 1,247 Python submissions from 382 CodeNet problems~\cite{puri2021project} and 181 Java submissions from 17 Introductory Programming (CS1) exercises~\cite{duan2024tiktoc}, against LSGen~\cite{dai2026learner} and the same base model used zero-shot. In terms of repair rate under a declared edit budget, \textsc{ZPDPatch} fixed 44.0\% of the Python submissions at 20 Abstract Syntax Tree (AST) operations against LSGen's 13.9\%, and 14.7 points more on average across six budgets. Regarding patch size, its patches changed 21.05 AST nodes on average, less than a quarter of LSGen's 88.27. Under matched sources, Progress targets kept 5.6 and 6.5 percentage points more of the student's tokens on the inputs both rules fixed, although they fixed 19.2 and 11.2 points fewer. We further show the same two directions on student-disjoint CS1 Java, where Answer targets lead on 15 of 17 exercises, and that three fixed candidate draws alone account for 15.1 points of the repair rate. Unrestricted, however, no configuration of ours exceeded LSGen's 80.2\%, so we retain the current-only Answer triple and report the Progress rule as the option for when locality binds (Section~\ref{sec:experiment}).

\noindent\textbf{Contributions.} We summarize our contributions below:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Observation.} We show that solution-supervised repair seldom returns a step from where a student stands, and that within a step of 20 AST operations a trajectory target repairs 3.2 times as many submissions (Section~\ref{sec:limitation}), motivating repair that takes its target from the student's own trajectory.
\item \textbf{New technique.} We present \textsc{ZPDPatch}, a trajectory-supervised repair technique for programming assignments. Our core technical contribution is an algorithm that pairs each buggy submission with a later submission of its own trajectory under execution-evidence rules, trains one adapter per rule, and freezes a checkpoint triple by executed validation repairs, with the sources, the example counts and the candidate budget held fixed.
\item \textbf{Evaluation.} We demonstrate the effectiveness of \textsc{ZPDPatch} by comparing it with LSGen, a zero-shot model, and controls on the target's source, prompt, decoder and candidate budget: it changes the least of the student's program, while no configuration of ours leads unrestricted. We supply the paired outcomes, condition manifests, metric code, and a claim-to-evidence map as anonymous supplementary material.
\end{itemize}

\section{Preliminaries}
\label{sec:preliminaries}
\subsection{Automated Program Repair for Programming Assignments}
\label{sec:formulation}

Existing techniques for repairing programming assignments aim to turn a buggy submission into a program that passes every test of its problem. A problem \(p\) comes with a problem context \(d_p\), its statement and resource limits, and a test suite \(\mathcal E\). That aim asks only whether a return passes the tests, and nothing about what the student submitted. We define the rest of the setting on the student's submissions themselves. A student working on \(p\) submits repeatedly. We define a submission as a triple \(s=(c,v,o)\), where \(c\) represents its source code, \(v\) denotes the verdict the platform recorded, and \(o\) indicates execution evidence that sends each test of \(\mathcal E\) to its re-executed outcome. The recorded verdict and the replayed outcome stay in separate fields, because runtime variability can change what is observed. The \emph{trajectory} \(\tau=[s_1,\ldots,s_n,s_{\mathit{ac}}]\) orders those states chronologically, with \(s_1,\ldots,s_n\) buggy and \(s_{\mathit{ac}}\) the first accepted submission, which serves only as evaluation ground truth and never reaches a technique. The submission to be repaired is the latest buggy one, and we call it the \emph{buggy program} \(b=s_n\). Cutting each \(\tau\) at its first acceptance yields the \emph{canonical} dataset and its frozen split.

\begin{algorithm}[h]
\caption{APR for Programming Assignments}
\label{alg:generic}
\footnotesize
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Buggy program ($b$), Context ($x$), Ordered generators ($S$), Tests ($\mathcal E$), Edit budget ($B$)
\Ensure Returned program ($f$)
\Procedure{Repair}{$b, x, S, \mathcal E, B$}
\State $f\gets b$; $q^*\gets\textsc{Execute}(b,\mathcal E)$
\For{$g\in S$}
 \State $z\gets\mathsf{Generate}(g,x)$
 \If{$B<\infty$ and $\mathrm{ted}^{\dagger}(b,z)>B$}
  \State \textbf{continue}
 \EndIf
 \State $q\gets\textsc{Execute}(z,\mathcal E)$
 \If{$q=1$} \State \Return $z$ \EndIf
 \If{$(q,-\mathrm{ted}^{\dagger}(b,z))>(q^*,-\mathrm{ted}^{\dagger}(b,f))$}
  \State $f\gets z$; $q^*\gets q$
 \EndIf
\EndFor
\State \Return $f$
\EndProcedure
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

Algorithm~\ref{alg:generic} describes a generic repair procedure for programming assignments in the style of existing techniques~\cite{gulwani2018automated,wang2018search,hu2020re}. It takes five inputs, a buggy program \(b\), a context \(x\), an ordered set \(S\) of generators, a test suite \(\mathcal E\), and an edit budget \(B\), and returns one program \(f\). Line~2 makes the buggy program \(b\) the fallback for the return \(f\) and uses \(\textsc{Execute}\) to record in \(q^*\) the fraction \(q\) of tests that \(b\) passes. Lines~3--4 take each generator \(g\) of the ordered set \(S\) in turn, and \(\mathsf{Generate}\) builds one complete candidate \(z\) from the context \(x\). Lines~5--6 skip a candidate whose \(\mathrm{ted}^{\dagger}\) from \(b\) exceeds the edit budget \(B\). Here \(\mathrm{ted}^{\dagger}\) is AST edit distance when both programs parse, zero for unchanged code, and infinity otherwise. An unrestricted \(B\) therefore admits syntax repair. Line~7 executes the candidate and lines~8--9 return it as soon as every test passes. Lines~10--11 replace \(f\) with a candidate that passes a larger fraction, or the same fraction from closer to \(b\), and line~12 returns \(f\) when no candidate passes. For now, let us set aside what the context \(x\) holds and where \(S\) and \(B\) come from; all three are fixed in Section~\ref{sec:approach}.

Existing techniques instantiate Algorithm~\ref{alg:generic} in different ways. Alignment-based techniques replace the generator at line~4 with an alignment against reference programs. CLARA~\cite{gulwani2018automated} uses the expressions of a reference cluster sharing the control flow of \(b\), Sarfgen~\cite{wang2018search} aligns same-control-flow references on their ASTs, Refactory~\cite{hu2020re} refactors references toward the control flow of \(b\), and assignment-aware techniques vary how references are chosen~\cite{heo2023referent,choi2021automated,choi2025automated}. Language-model-based techniques~\cite{joshi2023repair,zhang2024pydex,phung2023generating,liu2024fastfixer,yang2024cref,zhao2024peer,orvalho2025counterexample} replace line~4 and often the generator set at line~3 with a model. Retrieval-based LSGen~\cite{dai2026learner} keeps that model and retrieves a learner-specific solution into the context \(x\). Next-step hint generators~\cite{piech2015embeddings,rivers2017datadriven,price2017isnap,roest2024next} return one edit rather than a program, so neither line~7 nor line~5 applies to them. In every case the generator is steered toward an accepted solution or a solution space, never toward the later submissions of the student being helped.

\subsection{Limitation of Solution-Supervised Repair}
\label{sec:limitation}

\begin{figure}[h]
\centering
\includegraphics{Figures/icse-seet-student-reach.pdf}
\caption{Students' own steps and LSGen's returns within each budget}
\label{fig:reach}
\end{figure}

Our key observation is that solution-supervised repair does not return a step from where the student stands. A return that replaces most of \(b\) is not a continuation of it, whether or not the student could reach it with guidance. Locating a student's regions takes measurement of its own (Section~\ref{sec:introduction}), so we count how much of \(b\) the return keeps. We measure it by the \(\mathrm{ted}^{\dagger}\) of Algorithm~\ref{alg:generic} and by token retention (Section~\ref{sec:experiment}). More precisely, we call a return whose distance from \(b\) is at most a declared budget \(B\) a \emph{bounded step}, where \(B\) is the amount of a student's program a course is willing to give up to one repair.

Figure~\ref{fig:reach} measures how large a step the students themselves take and what LSGen repairs within it, over the 997 Seen inputs. On Seen the 2,581 steps with a defined distance have a median of 15, and one step leaves a median of 96.1\% of the submission's tokens in place. Unrestricted, the returns of LSGen sit at 88.27 and those of the zero-shot model at 27.25. We call the largest step a student took unaided on a problem their \emph{demonstrated reach}. Its median is 39, with quartiles at 15 and 83. An average LSGen return is 2.5 times an average student step and larger than the biggest step the median student took alone. Twenty AST operations is close to the median student step and within the reach of 30.9\% of students, where LSGen repairs 13.9\%, and 160 covers the reach of 90.3\% of students. Surprisingly, four out of five of the submissions LSGen can repair are not repaired within one step, since it repairs 80.2\% of them when nothing bounds the step. This measurement counts what a student changes at once, not which steps lay inside that student's region.

Prior techniques already ask for a small change: Refactory synthesizes minimal repairs~\cite{hu2020re} and LSGen instructs the model to make minimal changes~\cite{dai2026learner}. One alternative is to bound the step of an existing technique. The 13.9\% above is that alternative, and it costs LSGen 66.3 of its 80.2 points, because a generator steered toward an accepted solution has no smaller candidate to fall back on. Returning a hint sidesteps the budget, but the student still has to write the step, and the hint is still fitted to a solution space~\cite{rivers2017datadriven,price2017isnap,roest2024next}. Another alternative is to swap the target for the student's own later submission and leave everything else alone. That is not a one-variable intervention: the same trajectories yield 40,454 Answer examples but 16,973 and 21,416 under Strict and Progress, and the source frequencies move too. What a rule retains moves with it too: among three wrong-answer submissions with pass vectors \((1,0,0)\), \((1,1,0)\), and \((0,1,1)\), verdict-only filtering cannot tell the second from the third, while test-wise progress retains the second, which keeps every passed test and gains one, and not the third, which gains one but loses another. Extra candidates also raise the repair rate on their own: three fixed draws alone account for 15.1 points (Section~\ref{sec:experiment}), so a comparison that does not hold the candidate budget fixed credits the target with them~\cite{zhang2024pydex,koutcheme2024benchmarking,yang2024cref,orvalho2025counterexample,dai2026learner}.

These limitations motivate our approach, which repairs under the guidance of the student's own trajectory. The goal of this paper is to repair a buggy program into one that passes the tests while keeping as much of the submission as possible. In terms of Algorithm~\ref{alg:generic}, we train the generator toward the student's own later submission rather than toward an accepted solution or a solution space. A later submission is a program the student actually produced, so it is reachable by construction, but it need not be correct; we therefore use it as a preference and let the tests decide what is returned. Our work thus tackles two challenges, (1) that the student's later submission marks the next step only coarsely and may itself be accepted, and (2) that attributing a gain to the target requires holding the sources, the example counts, and the candidate budget fixed.

\section{Our Approach}
\label{sec:approach}

\subsection{Overview}

\begin{algorithm}[h]
\caption{\textsc{ZPDPatch}}
\label{alg:construct}
\footnotesize
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Trajectories ($\mathcal T$), Execution evidence ($\mathcal O$), Validation inputs ($U$), Seeds ($\Sigma$), Portfolio size ($\eta_k$), Edit budget ($B$)
\Ensure Frozen ordered checkpoint portfolio ($S$)
\Procedure{ZPDPatch}{$\mathcal T, \mathcal O, U, \Sigma, \eta_k, B$}
 \Statex\hspace{\algorithmicindent}\textit{/* Stage 1. Mine */}
\State $(D_A,D_S,D_P)\gets(\emptyset,\emptyset,\emptyset)$
\For{$\tau=[s_1,\ldots,s_n]\in\mathcal T$}
 \State $D_A\gets D_A\cup\mathsf{TerminalPairs}(\tau)$ \Comment{Answer}
 \For{$\rho\in\{S,P\}$}
  \State $R\gets[s_1]$ \Comment{retained prefix}
  \For{$s_t\in[s_2,\ldots,s_n]$}
   \If{$\mathsf{Improves}_{\rho}(R[-1],s_t;\mathcal O)$}
    \State $D_\rho\gets D_\rho\cup\{(R,c_t)\}$; $R\gets R\mathbin{\|}[s_t]$
   \EndIf
  \EndFor
 \EndFor
\EndFor
 \Statex\hspace{\algorithmicindent}\textit{/* Stage 2. Train */}
\State $V\gets\emptyset$
\For{$(\rho,\sigma)\in\{A,S,P\}\times\Sigma$}
 \State $V\gets V\cup\{\mathsf{Train}(D_\rho,\sigma)\}$
\EndFor
 \Statex\hspace{\algorithmicindent}\textit{/* Stage 3. Select */}
\For{$g\in V$}
 \State $R_g^B\gets\mathsf{Validate}(g,U,B)$ \Comment{$R_g^B\subseteq U$}
\EndFor
\State $S\gets\mathsf{Select}(\{R_g^B\}_{g\in V},\eta_k)$ \Comment{$\eta_k=3$}
\State \Return $\mathsf{FreezeAndOrder}(S)$
\EndProcedure
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

Figure~\ref{fig:overview} illustrates how \textsc{ZPDPatch} repairs a buggy program under the guidance of the student's own trajectory. \textsc{ZPDPatch} takes the inputs of Algorithm~\ref{alg:generic}, namely the buggy program \(b\), the context \(x\), and the test suite \(\mathcal E\), together with the trajectories \(\mathcal T\) of a training corpus and their execution evidence \(\mathcal O\). It processes these inputs in the three stages of Algorithm~\ref{alg:construct}, namely Mine, Train, and Select. The Mine stage pairs each buggy submission of a \(\tau\in\mathcal T\), or a retained prefix of it, with a later submission under three target rules, yielding one dataset per rule (Section~\ref{sec:mine}). The Train stage trains one adapter per rule and seed, giving a pool of checkpoints (Section~\ref{sec:train}). The Select stage runs each checkpoint on validation inputs and freezes the ordered triple whose executed repairs cover the most of them (Section~\ref{sec:select}). At deployment the frozen checkpoints are the generator set \(S\) of Algorithm~\ref{alg:generic}, which returns the first candidate that passes every test and otherwise returns the submission unchanged.

\textsc{ZPDPatch} differs from existing techniques in where it obtains the target and in what it holds fixed around it. First, the target is the student's own later submission. Existing techniques supervise the generator toward an accepted solution~\cite{gulwani2018automated,wang2018search,hu2020re}, a solution space built from past correct programs~\cite{piech2015embeddings,rivers2017datadriven,price2017isnap}, or a retrieved learner-specific solution~\cite{dai2026learner}, so what a return aims at is settled without the student's own evidence; the target of \textsc{ZPDPatch} is the next state of the same trajectory that execution evidence shows to be an improvement. Second, because swapping the target also moves the training population and the candidate budget (Section~\ref{sec:limitation}), \textsc{ZPDPatch} holds the sources, the example counts, and the candidate budget fixed across rules and admits a matched-peer target as a control, so a difference in outcome can be read as the target's. The three stages run once per corpus, and deployment issues at most one call per selected checkpoint, so the deployed budget is three calls.

\subsection{\textup{\textsc{ZPDPatch}}}

Algorithm~\ref{alg:construct} details how \textsc{ZPDPatch} instantiates Algorithm~\ref{alg:generic}. Taking \(\mathcal T\) and \(\mathcal O\) in addition to the inputs of Algorithm~\ref{alg:generic}, it builds the generator set offline and requires no instructor solution, no pool of reference programs, and no learner model.

At lines~2--9, Mine scans each trajectory \(\tau\) and emits the pairs of each rule (Section~\ref{sec:mine}). The Answer rule pairs every non-accepted state with the accepted endpoint (line~4), whereas the Strict and Progress rules carry a retained prefix \(R\) and append a state only when the rule's improvement predicate holds on the execution evidence \(\mathcal O\) (lines~7--9), emitting \((R,c_t)\) before the state is appended. At lines~10--12, Train fits one adapter per rule and seed, so the pool \(V\) holds \(|\Sigma|\) checkpoints per rule (Section~\ref{sec:train}). At lines~13--16, Select runs each checkpoint on the validation inputs \(U\) under the edit budget \(B\), records the inputs it repairs, and freezes the ordered triple whose union covers the most of \(U\), where \(\eta_k=3\) in our experiments (Section~\ref{sec:select}). Choosing by executed validation repairs rather than by training loss ties the choice to the only outcome a deployment can observe. The returned portfolio \(S\) is what line~3 of Algorithm~\ref{alg:generic} iterates over at deployment.

The order of \(S\) affects which passing program a student receives, not whether one is found, because every checkpoint of \(S\) is invoked until a candidate passes and the gate returns \(b\) unchanged when none does. Hence, given deterministic test outcomes, the repair set of the portfolio contains that of each of its members.

\subsection{Mine}
\label{sec:mine}

The goal of the Mine stage is to make every supervision pair traceable to an observed source and a later submission (lines~2--9). Figure~\ref{fig:example} shows what that choice changes on one trajectory: the Progress target (b) is one AST operation from (a), while the Answer target (c) is 33 operations away and takes another approach. Submissions are grouped by problem and user, chronologically ordered, and cut at the first acceptance. Canonical normalization removes consecutive duplicates while preserving the source code used for generation and execution. A whole-trajectory filter instead excludes a trajectory before splitting if any required prompt--target configuration exceeds 4,096 tokens. Exclusion changes the evaluated population, but never truncates a program or history.

The coarse severity function assigns 0 to Accepted (AC), 1 to Wrong Answer (WA), 2 to Time or Memory Limit Exceeded (TLE/MLE), 3 to Runtime Error (RE), and 4 to Compile Error (CE); unrecognized and internal outcomes occupy the most severe category. Strict compares recorded verdicts. Progress additionally compares replayed outcomes when the recorded verdict strings agree. Its cache must be complete, and compared vectors must have equal, nonempty testcase keys.

For equal testcase universes, \(o_t\prec_\times o_r\) holds when every test in \(o_t\) has severity no greater than its counterpart in \(o_r\), with a strict decrease on at least one. Formally, the improvement predicates are:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\mathsf{Improves}_S(r,t)&\iff \mathrm{sev}(v_t)<\mathrm{sev}(v_r),\\
\mathsf{Improves}_P(r,t)&\iff \mathsf{Improves}_S(r,t)\\
 &\quad\lor\ (v_t=v_r\land o_t\prec_\times o_r).
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:progress}
\end{equation}
Intuitively, Equation~\eqref{eq:progress} retains coarse improvements and progress hidden by an unchanged verdict. A severity improvement such as RE to WA need not increase the pass fraction, and a verdict-improving edge does not require test-wise dominance.

Answer emits an independent pair for each non-AC source and the accepted endpoint. Thus \([s_1,\ldots,s_9]\) yields \(([s_1],c_9),\ldots,([s_8],c_9)\); it does not expose accumulated prefixes to Answer training. Strict and Progress start with \(R=[s_1]\), compare each later state with the \emph{last retained} state, and append it only when the predicate holds. Before appending \(s_t\), the builder emits \((R,c_t)\). For WA, improved WA, AC, Strict emits \(([s_1],c_3)\), whereas Progress emits \(([s_1],c_2)\) and \(([s_1,s_2],c_3)\). The accepted endpoint remains eligible in both rules. Although Strict's pairwise predicate is contained in Progress's, their independently scanned edge sets need not be nested.

Chronological indices strictly increase, so construction terminates without cycles. With fixed-key evidence, retained edges also decrease the appropriate verdict/test severity rank. These are builder invariants, not evidence of a learned ZPD effect. Binary accepted/non-accepted mining retains 14,027 of 16,973 Strict pairs and 18,009 of 21,416 Progress pairs. Models retrained on them agree with the canonical ones on 84.6\% and 87.5\% of Seen repair decisions (Section~\ref{sec:generality}), so we read the ranking as operational rather than universal.

\subsection{Train}
\label{sec:train}
The goal of the Train stage is to learn each relation independently while keeping model and optimization comparable (lines~10--12). The implementation fine-tunes Qwen2.5-Coder-7B-Instruct with 4-bit Quantized Low-Rank Adaptation (QLoRA)~\cite{hui2024qwen2,dettmers2023qlora}. Adapters have rank 16, scaling 32, and dropout 0.05, targeting attention and feed-forward projections. Canonical training uses one epoch, learning rate \(2\times10^{-4}\), and seeds 2027--2029. Assistant target tokens supply cross-entropy loss. The role-neutral prompt contains the statement, limits, permitted submissions, execution/edit summaries, and a request for a complete solution. It names neither the rule nor a preferred patch size, so specialization must come from the data rather than explicit instructions.

Targets are future submissions available offline; inference inputs contain only observed states. Current-only controls reset positions and remove earlier entries, preventing length leakage through position labels. Answer training is already single-source, so supplying history at inference creates a distribution change. The history-removal retraining control instead holds targets fixed and changes earlier-submission serialization. Source-matched target training holds current input and count fixed.

The paired-target control inner-joins records by problem, user, submission identifier, and the exact buggy program, retaining sources with distinct Answer and Progress targets. Its 7,389 training and 931 validation examples per condition use identical current-only inputs. The separate peer control requires another user on the same problem and in the same split, with exactly equal target execution evidence. Writing \(y_u\) for the same-user target and \(y_v\) for its peer replacement, for semantic-token diff distance \(d\) the discrepancy \(\delta=|d(c,y_v)-d(c,y_u)|\) must satisfy \(\delta\le2\) or \(\delta/\max(1,d(c,y_u))\le0.1\). Scarce evidence groups are visited first; ties prioritize distance discrepancy, reuse, and identifier, with donor reuse capped at three. This greedy rule yields 5,231 training and 306 validation examples per condition. Equal outcomes and nearby distances do not equate structure or style, so its measured effect belongs to the entire replacement rule, not isolated provenance.

\subsection{Select}
\label{sec:select}
The goal of the Select stage is to deploy a bounded portfolio chosen without final-test outcomes (lines~13--16). For validation inputs \(U\), let \(R_v^B\) be inputs repaired by checkpoint \(v\) within edit budget \(B\). Formally, selection maximizes:
\begin{equation}
 f_B(S)=\frac{1}{|U|}\left|\bigcup_{v\in S} R_v^B\right|,\qquad |S|=3.
\label{eq:portfolio}
\end{equation}
Intuitively, Equation~\eqref{eq:portfolio} rewards complementary successes, not low training loss or the presence of every relation. Validation uses one fixed-hash trajectory from each of 461 eligible problems. The mixed pool has nine checkpoints and 84 triples; requiring one checkpoint per relation instead permits 27. The primary unconstrained mixed choice is Answer2027, Answer2028, and Progress2027. The relation-constrained choice is a different condition. Answer-9Choose3 receives nine Answer checkpoints and the same 84-triple search. Ties use recorded pass-rate, improvement, and deterministic identifier priorities. Budget-indexed selection repeats the declared objective at \(B\in\{5,10,20,40,80,160\}\), without choosing a budget from test results.

Selected checkpoints and order are frozen. Each emits at most one independent candidate, and the first observed acceptance stops generation. If none passes all tests, Algorithm~\ref{alg:generic} prioritizes pass fraction and then smaller distance. Because unchanged code is feasible, for the same fixed execution measurements the returned pass fraction cannot decrease, and a finite edit budget cannot be exceeded. This is not per-test non-regression: equal fractions can conceal gains and losses on different tests. It also does not guarantee non-regression under fresh execution, an unseen suite, or resource variability.

Terminal pairing is linear in trajectory length; replaying \(n\) submissions on \(m\) tests requires \(O(nm)\) executions and evidence storage. The 84-triple search is bounded at our pool size, not a scalable optimizer.

\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiment}
In this section, we evaluate \textsc{ZPDPatch} to answer the following research questions:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{RQ1: Repair Effectiveness.} Which configuration provides the strongest unrestricted repair performance?
\item \textbf{RQ2: Target Construction and Locality.} How do matched targets affect correctness and locality, and how do both distribute across exercises and students?
\item \textbf{RQ3: Component Efficacy.} What do candidate breadth, checkpoint identity, and serialized history contribute?
\item \textbf{RQ4: Generality.} Which findings replicate across problems, prefixes, scales, and Java populations?
\end{itemize}
We implemented \textsc{ZPDPatch} on top of Qwen2.5-Coder-7B-Instruct~\cite{hui2024qwen2}, an open code model of a scale a course can host. All experiments were conducted on a single machine equipped with one NVIDIA RTX 5090.

\subsection{RQ2: Target Construction and Locality}
\label{sec:rq-locality}

RQ2 asks how the target rule changes correctness and locality, and how both distribute across exercises and students. To this end, we replaced the target while holding the sources and the example counts fixed, and repeated the comparison on student-held-out Java.

\noindent\textbf{Matched targets.} Table~\ref{tab:targets} changes the target while holding current inputs and training counts fixed. Progress-minus-Answer RR is -19.2 points [-22.96, -15.54] on Seen and -11.2 [-17.37, -4.88] on Unseen, with exact McNemar \(p=7.85\times10^{-34}\) and \(p=6.17\times10^{-5}\). The Answer dataset's advantage is therefore not only more training records. The paired population is restricted to sources with distinct targets, and a Progress target can itself be accepted, so the intervention is the target rule rather than a clean incorrect-versus-correct contrast. Under the matched inputs the Progress rule repairs 34.0\% of Seen inputs and improves 43.4\%, so a further 9.4 points receive a program that advances without passing, against 53.2\% and 65.0\% for the Answer rule. The same two gaps are 6.4 and 7.6 points on Unseen, and 9.4 against 2.2 on Java, where Answer targets already repair 85.6\% outright. Part of the coverage the Progress rule gives up therefore returns as partial progress, counted by test execution and not by comprehension. The cause lies in the target itself: a Progress target is not required to pass, so the generator trained on it returns programs that advance without passing.

Progress's locality benefit is visible on joint successes. Among 299 Seen and 154 Unseen jointly repaired inputs, 278 and 134 have defined source-to-repair TED for both methods. Answer-minus-Progress mean TED is 10.08 [7.53, 12.99] and 6.83 [4.58, 9.39]. Token retention improves by 5.6 [4.29, 6.94] and 6.5 [4.26, 8.95] points; line retention improves by 8.9 [6.64, 11.16] and 10.4 [6.95, 14.25]. The 21 and 20 undefined-TED cases remain in RR and are not declared unrepairable. These measures describe successful outputs that retain more of the submission, not easier comprehension.

Submission (a) passes 93.3\% of tests after the student's own preceding edit changed \texttt{i<j} to \texttt{i<=j}, the Progress-target repair differs from it only in \texttt{j>0} becoming \texttt{j>=0} (TED 1), and the Answer-target repair (b) replaces the approach (TED 83). Both pass every test under the same gate and budget.

\noindent\textbf{Peer targets.} The separate outcome- and distance-matched replacement raises RR from 50.4\% to 54.7\% on Seen and from 78.4\% to 84.4\% on Unseen. Same-minus-peer intervals are [-7.71, -0.84] and [-11.79, -0.41], with exact McNemar \(p=0.005\) and \(p=0.032\). The result concerns this caliper and replacement rule, and equal outcomes with nearby distances still permit differences in structure, so the contrast does not isolate user identity. Matched peer targets improve coverage here; one's own trajectory is not uniquely necessary under this control.

\noindent\textbf{Operational locality.} Per-budget Seen rates are 16.9/22.5/4.8\% at TED 5, 28.5/32.4/8.0 at 10, 40.3/44.0/13.9 at 20, 51.4/50.9/30.4 at 40, 61.0/56.0/50.8 at 80, and 66.8/57.9/67.6 at 160 for current-only Answer-3Seed/Mixed/LSGen, each over 997 inputs. At TED 20, Mixed repairs 44.0\% of Seen inputs versus LSGen's 13.9\%, a 30.1-point difference [26.45, 33.82]. Across the six prespecified budgets its mean advantage over LSGen is 14.7 points [12.03, 17.43]. This is a budget-indexed outcome, not an unrestricted superiority claim: LSGen wins at 160, and current-only Answer-3Seed overtakes Mixed from 40 onward. On Unseen, Mixed versus current-only Answer-3Seed is 38.8\% versus 32.0\% at 5, 48.0\% versus 40.8\% at 10, and 57.6\% versus 54.8\% at 20; the order reverses at 40. Overall, target construction moves coverage and locality in opposite directions under matched inputs, in the same direction on student-held-out Java, and not uniformly across exercises or students, and matched peer targets also improve coverage, so the effect is not uniquely personal supervision.

\subsection{RQ3: Component Efficacy}

RQ3 asks what candidate breadth, checkpoint identity, and serialized history each contribute. To this end, we compared controls that change one of them at a time within the stored executions.

\noindent\textbf{Fixed candidate breadth.} For three stored draws with success indicators \(a_{ij}\), the expected uniformly selected one-draw success for input \(i\) is \(\frac{1}{3}\sum_j a_{ij}\), whereas the three-draw union succeeds when \(\max_j a_{ij}=1\). Their paired difference isolates additional opportunities within the identical candidate set. Replacing greedy decoding by one stochastic draw costs 7.6 points [-9.81, -5.41], whereas execution-selecting three of those identical fixed draws contributes 15.1 points [13.48, 16.76] on Seen. It shows why comparing a portfolio only with one adapter overstates the evidence for its training signal.

The temperature sweep retains all declared conditions. Through \(T=1.0\) the one-draw rate stays within 41.1--43.1\% on Seen, the three-draw union reaches 60.0/75.2\%, and at \(T=1.5\) the decoder collapses to 15.8/35.2\%. At \(T=0.8\), twenty draws reach 74.8\% on Seen and 84.0\% on Unseen but invoke 7.44 and 5.08 calls on average, so those rates do not replace the three-call primary results. Fine-tuning also matters: the matched base-model three-draw union reaches only 30.4/65.6\%, so adaptation adds 27.2 points [22.99, 31.41] on Seen and 6.4 points [1.19, 11.34] on Unseen. Breadth alone therefore does not explain all improvement over the base model.

At matched temperature, replacing three draws of one checkpoint by one draw from each of three checkpoints changes RR by -0.1 points [-2.00, 1.80] on Seen and 2.4 points [-0.41, 5.31] on Unseen. These intervals do not establish a consistent advantage from checkpoint diversity. They also do not show equivalence. A deterministic multi-seed gain can coexist with this result because sampling from one checkpoint already supplies diversity.

A current-only factorial on one inference stack tests this explanation. Three greedy checkpoints gain 9.8 [7.85, 11.92] and 10.8 [7.23, 14.64] points over one, but under the matched stochastic decoder distinct checkpoints add only 2.1 points [0.00, 4.16] and 1.2 points [-2.93, 5.93]. The equal-problem Seen interval includes zero, so this does not establish a robust checkpoint benefit. Absolute Seen/Unseen rates on that stack are 53.1/67.2\% for one greedy checkpoint, 62.9/78.0\% for three greedy checkpoints, 66.8/79.6\% for a same-checkpoint stochastic triple, and 68.9/80.8\% for a distinct-checkpoint triple, against 72.0/81.2\% on the historical one. Backend sensitivity is material, so the recommendation is restricted to the evaluated stack.

\noindent\textbf{Serialized history.} Removing earlier submissions from the frozen triples raises repair for both pools. The gains are 8.8/11.1 Seen points and 7.6/6.0 Unseen points for Mixed/Answer, with all intervals excluding zero. Matched retraining without that context shows no significant difference after Holm correction, with smallest adjusted \(p=0.313\), so serialized history is not part of the measured advantage and the deployed configuration uses current-only prompts.

Answer-9Choose3 receives the same nine-checkpoint pool size and 84-subset opportunity as Mixed. The primary five-fold cross-fitted mixed-minus-Answer contrast is +2.2 points [-0.19, 4.63], compared with the non-cross-fitted +1.7 [-0.79, 4.29]. These estimates are positive but uncertain. Overall, candidate breadth and adaptation each contribute measured gains, whereas distinct checkpoints add no consistent advantage once the stochastic decoder is matched and serialized history is not supported as a necessary input.

\subsection{RQ4: Generality}
\label{sec:generality}
RQ4 asks which findings replicate across problems, prefixes, model scales, and Java populations. To this end, we measured the same contrasts again on held-out problems, earlier prefixes, a smaller model, and the classroom corpus.

\noindent\textbf{Problems and execution suites.} Within Unseen, Mixed exceeds Zero-shot by 12.8 points [6.83, 18.75], with 45 versus 13 exclusive successes and exact McNemar \(p=3.01\times10^{-5}\). Among 142 joint repairs, 122 have defined TED for both methods; on those 122 it reduces TED by 6.41 [3.43, 9.89]. The higher absolute Unseen RR is not evidence of stronger generalization: the partitions differ in availability and composition. Matching all 54 Unseen problems to distinct Seen problems on six pre-repair difficulty covariates changes the raw gap into -4.6 equal-problem points [-14.18, 5.30]. We therefore rely on within-split paired contrasts rather than the raw cross-split difference.

A complementary check partitions the existing authored suite. On Unseen, Mixed and Answer-9Choose3 jointly pass observed and held-back slices on 189/250 and 184/250 inputs, respectively. Conditional confirmation among observed-suite successes is 189/192 (98.4\%) and 184/186 (98.9\%); 13 versus 8 exclusive joint successes yield \(p=0.383\). Repeating the procedure on all 328 Seen problems with a distinct seed yields 97.8\% and 97.9\% confirmation, but joint success is 452/997 versus 469/997. High confirmation thus does not demonstrate that Mixed wins. These are held-back slices of the same authored tests, so the conditional percentage must not replace the full-denominator repair rate.

\noindent\textbf{Earlier prefixes.} Across 2,770 Seen and 674 Unseen executable prefixes of eventually successful trajectories, current-only Answer-3Seed reaches 69.6\% and 80.1\% RR. The early, middle, and last thirds reach 70.0\%, 67.6\%, and 71.4\% on Seen and 82.9\%, 77.3\%, and 81.8\% on Unseen. It extends evaluation beyond the last failed submission and finds no monotonic position effect. It does not cover trajectories that never reach acceptance and multiple prefixes from one trajectory are dependent, so the 2,770-prefix rate is not added to the 997-input result.

\noindent\textbf{Model scale and classroom data.} At 1.5B parameters three checkpoints still gain 13.4 points over one [11.16, 15.92], but mixed-minus-Answer is -0.8 [-2.62, 1.07]. Mixed reaches 50.25\% and the nine-checkpoint Answer control 51.05\% on Seen. This smaller model reproduces the utility of multiple opportunities, not an unrestricted mixed-target advantage.

The Java portfolio results differ from RQ2's direct matched-target retraining. Mixed-minus-Answer-9Choose3 is positive on four held-out exercises (3.9 points [2.99, 5.11]) and inconclusive on held-out students (0.6 [-1.20, 2.59]). Absolute RR is 56.25\% versus 52.30\% over 304 exercise-held-out trajectories and 83.98\% versus 83.43\% over 181 student-held-out trajectories. The exercise estimate rests on four clusters: refitting with one exercise left out keeps the contrast between 3.6 and 4.4 points, and single exercises range from 2.5 to 5.5 points. Four clusters cannot support a broad classroom-population claim.

\noindent\textbf{Representation and overlap.} Binary accepted/non-accepted mining removes the fine severity ordering but also changes the target set. Retraining changes Progress RR by +2.3 Seen and -2.8 Unseen points in the canonical-minus-alternative direction. The nonuniform changes show that the ordering is not an innocuous implementation detail. For Mixed and Answer-9Choose3, exact matches between a returned repair and a training-time accepted program occur in 0.6\% and 1.3\% of selections, near-duplicates above a 0.90 token-similarity threshold in 3.8\% and 5.3\%, and median maximum similarity to a same-problem training target is 66.7\% and 68.6\%. Low exact-copy rates do not exclude structural reuse or pretraining contamination~\cite{allamanis2019adverse}. Overall, the matched-target tradeoff replicates on student-held-out Java and candidate breadth remains useful at a smaller model scale, whereas mixed-target unrestricted superiority does not replicate consistently.

\subsection{Discussion}

\noindent\textbf{Implications for instructors.} Our results suggest three ways a course can use these repairs. First, declaring an edit budget still leaves repairs to show: within 20 AST operations our configuration repairs 44.0\% of the Seen inputs where LSGen repairs 13.9\% (Section~\ref{sec:rq-locality}). Second, which rule a course deploys follows from whether coverage or locality binds, since the Progress rule keeps more of the student's tokens on the inputs both rules fix and repairs fewer of them (Section~\ref{sec:rq-locality}). Third, that choice is better made per exercise than for a whole course, because the coverage cost concentrates on particular exercises and the retention gain reaches about a third of the students who receive a repair (Section~\ref{sec:rq-locality}).

\noindent\textbf{Delivering repairs.} A returned program can be shown as it is, reduced to the lines it changed, or passed to a hint generator as input~\cite{phung2023generating,roest2024next}. Since handing a model a solution steers its hints toward that solution, a return that keeps more of the student's own approach is likely the better input for such hints as well. Which form of delivery helps learning most remains to be studied with students.

\subsection{Threats to Validity}
(1) Runtime variability, undefined source ASTs, and Python whitespace affect labels and distances. We retain unknowns, denominators, and whitespace-sensitive corrections, but hashes establish provenance, not correctness, and source and peer matching do not equate all target semantics or styles. (2) Acceptance is not proof of semantic correctness~\cite{smith2015cure}, same-suite slices are not independent specifications, and TED and retention do not measure cognitive load or subsequent learning. CodeNet does not identify novices or measure mastery, Java supplies classroom submissions rather than an intervention observing feedback use, and the per-exercise and per-student resolution describes which students would receive a different repair, not which students benefit, with Figure~\ref{fig:case} one post-hoc case that carries no estimate. We display a CodeNet program because the course data terms do not permit redistributing student submissions. (3) Filtering favors short, eventually successful Python trajectories and permits Seen user overlap, Java adds one course context and only four held-out exercises, and cluster bootstrap and equal-problem estimates do not eliminate small-cluster uncertainty, multiplicity, or the bias of analyses chosen after earlier results. These distinctions limit educational and target-quality interpretations.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}
\noindent\textbf{Repair and Generation Budgets.} Generate-and-validate repair spans search, learned transformations, and language models~\cite{gazzola2019automatic,monperrus2018automatic,weimer2009automatically,legoues2012genprog}. Search analyses~\cite{qi2014strength} and execution-based evaluation~\cite{haque2023fixeval,chen2021evaluating} separate candidate quality from exposure. Neural patches, ensembles, synthetic corruption, and execution-informed self-supervision provide different training signals~\cite{tufano2019empirical,lutellier2020coconut,yasunaga2021break,ye2022selfapr}; zero-shot studies examine repair without task adaptation~\cite{xia2022less,jiang2023impact}. We do not introduce portfolio repair; we isolate trajectory targets with same-target seeds, equal search spaces, fixed draws, and current-only controls.

\noindent\textbf{Programming-Assignment Feedback.} Educational repair exploits correct programs through clustering, alignment, and refactoring~\cite{gulwani2018automated,wang2018search,hu2020re}; related representations and tutoring systems propagate feedback and generate hints~\cite{piech2015embeddings,glassman2015overcode,rivers2017datadriven,price2017isnap}. Language models support repair, syntax feedback, conversations, peers, and counterexamples~\cite{joshi2023repair,zhang2024pydex,phung2023generating,liu2024fastfixer,yang2024cref,zhao2024peer,orvalho2025counterexample}. Assignment-aware and diversified feedback use further reference strategies~\cite{heo2023referent,choi2021automated,choi2025automated}. LSGen also mines chronological wrong-to-later-correct submission pairs for retrieval~\cite{dai2026learner}, so trajectories are not our novelty, and it retains its strong Seen advantage here. It does not separate the history's contribution from that of extra candidates, however, which is why we hold the history and the candidate budget fixed together. Next-step hint generators differ from this work in what their guidance is fitted to. Data-driven hints read a solution space or a past cohort's paths and project the submission onto it, so two students at the same position in that space receive the same next step independently of which tests their own code currently passes~\cite{rivers2017datadriven,price2017isnap,roest2024next}. Our target rule reads the submission's own execution evidence instead and returns a complete program rather than a textual hint, so the two kinds of output are measured on different scales~\cite{yi2017feasibility,koutcheme2024benchmarking,keuning2018systematic}. Our narrower intervention replaces the target for matched sources and exposes a coverage--locality tradeoff, not dominance over feedback strategies.

\noindent\textbf{History and Learner Evidence.} ZPD and scaffolding concern supported performance, not program edit distance~\cite{cole1978mind,murray2002toward,chounta2017grey,schulze2024beyond}. Knowledge tracing models proficiency from interactions~\cite{corbett1994knowledge,piech2015deep,ghosh2020context}; TIKTOC incorporates testcase evidence~\cite{duan2024tiktoc}. Next-step hints, feedback ladders, and novice-response studies examine guidance more directly~\cite{roest2024next,heickal2024generating,xiao2024exploring}, while feedback theory emphasizes its interpretation and use~\cite{narciss2008feedback,hattie2007power,shute2008focus,aleven2000limitations}. This paper does not claim that conditioning on a learner's serialized history improves generated repairs. Our deployed configuration prompts with the current submission alone, and the supplement reports the matched retraining and prompt controls behind that choice, including their negative outcomes.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
Automated Program Repair has substantially raised the repair rate for buggy student submissions. However, existing techniques settle what a return aims at without reference to the student's own attempts, so a return can replace most of the submission. This paper aims to repair a buggy submission toward a step taken from the student's own trajectory, and to make the gain readable as the target's. To this end, \textsc{ZPDPatch} mines supervision pairs from execution evidence, trains one adapter per rule, and freezes a portfolio by executed validation repairs. Matched Python and student-held-out Java experiments show the same coverage--locality tradeoff, uneven across exercises and students, while candidate breadth, model adaptation and matched peer targets account for the rest of the improvement. We therefore retain the current-only Answer triple and report the Progress rule as the option for when locality binds, while educational benefit and broader model-family generality remain unmeasured.
